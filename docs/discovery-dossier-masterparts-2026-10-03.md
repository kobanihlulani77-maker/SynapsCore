# Founder Discovery Dossier — Masterparts
**Date:** 2026-10-03  
**Purpose:** Prepare a founder-level operational discovery conversation.  
**Status:** Public research only; no company-confirmed problem, sponsor or pilot.

## Why Masterparts remains important
Masterparts reports 60,000+ stocked items for 4,500+ vehicle models, 600+ staff and 16 branches. Gauteng includes a dedicated Modderfontein distribution centre plus Hennopspark, Montana, Randburg and a Boksburg location opening/operating in 2026. Account holders can use its online system, and Masterparts has customer-facing order tracking.

Masterparts itself explains that order tracking helps workshops estimate when customer jobs can be completed. That makes the downstream service consequence unusually tangible.

## Full-loop hypothesis
**Workshop/business demand -> correct part identity -> local branch/DC availability -> alternative supply -> competing urgency -> fulfillment decision -> dispatch/delivery -> workshop/customer outcome -> unresolved exception/recovery**

The central question is how the whole Gauteng network manages exceptions and priority across branch, DC and delivery—not whether Masterparts lacks ordering or tracking.

## What we need to learn
- ERP/WMS/order platform identities.
- What account ordering reads/writes.
- Inter-branch stock transfer or allocation process.
- Replenishment and procurement ownership.
- How urgent workshop/business requirements are represented.
- Who decides among competing stock commitments.
- How DC and branch handoffs are confirmed.
- Existing exception/control platform.
- Whether SkyNet/delivery status is visible internally alongside order/stock state.
- Data/export/API availability.

## Founder opening
Ask for a walkthrough of one urgent business-customer requirement that could not be fulfilled normally from the local branch.

Do not begin by telling them what their problem is.

## Seven high-value discovery questions
1. When the local branch cannot satisfy an urgent part request, what happens next?
2. Which systems show DC and other-branch options, and how current are they?
3. How are two competing urgent requests prioritized?
4. Who owns the case until the workshop/customer has the part?
5. How do staff know that a transfer, pick, dispatch or delivery actually occurred?
6. What happens when an order/delivery source becomes stale or fails?
7. What central tooling already gives management a cross-branch view of these cases?

## Evidence not to claim
- Do not infer the absence of ERP/WMS/control technology from public silence.
- Do not claim order tracking is internal operational orchestration.
- Do not claim workshops routinely suffer delays.
- Do not claim autonomous stock allocation or transfers are supported.
- Do not invent customer loss or revenue impact.

## Potential bounded pilot shape
Modderfontein DC + Hennopspark + Montana, optionally Randburg, with selected account-customer order flows and the operators responsible for exceptions.

This remains the cleanest physical pilot geometry found so far.

## Useful baseline metrics if company confirms relevance
- urgent-order investigation time;
- inter-site coordination handoffs;
- time to identify feasible alternative supply;
- decision-to-dispatch time;
- dispatch-to-confirmed-delivery time;
- unresolved exceptions at shift handover;
- customer status-update latency;
- source/integration recovery time.

## Disqualifiers for the proposed scope
- A mature existing cross-network control platform already handles the journey satisfactorily.
- No approved source/data path.
- Priority decisions are trivial/infrequent.
- Sponsor cannot isolate a regional scope.
- Value depends on unsupported direct allocation/transfer execution.
